### Getting Started

The starting phase can feel **overwhelming**. You’ll switch between different YouTube channels, understand one concept perfectly, and then suddenly feel completely lost in the next one. That’s normal.

For the **first month**, focus on building your fundamentals and solving easy problems. Stick to one channel that explains concepts in a beginner-friendly, spoon-fed way, such as **Love Babbar**, instead of constantly switching resources.

For practice, **LeetCode 75** is a great starting point. The problems are relatively beginner-friendly and help you build a solid base.

Once your fundamentals are strong, gradually reduce your dependency on tutorials and give most of your time towards **problems practicing**. You can then move towards **CSES** or **CP31**.

Two habits that will help you tremendously:

1. Prefer reading editorials over watching tutorials. Reading an editorial forces you to think through the solution yourself and gradually builds your problem-solving intuition.

2. Participate in contests and virtual contests frequently. Contests teach you how to think under pressure, manage time, choose which problems to attempt, and stay composed when a problem doesn't click immediately. Virtual contests are especially useful when you can't participate in a live contest, treat them like the real thing, without looking at solutions or editorials until the contest ends.

During this phase, start getting familiar with competitive programming resources and creators like [Errichto](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCBr_Fu6q9iHYQCh13jmpbrg), [William Lin](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCKuDLsO0Wwef53qdHPjbU2Q), and [Luv](https://www.youtube.com/@iamluv).

### Don't Chase Ratings

I wouldn't recommend chasing ratings or titles too early.

Suppose you start practicing problems around rating **x**, say `1100 < x < 1600`. Initially, the first 4–5 problems might feel difficult. But after solving around **35–40 problems** at that level, you'll start recognizing the patterns and the problems will feel much more familiar.

That's when you can gradually move towards **x + 100**.

Also, don't look at your current rating as the absolute limit of your ability. If your rating is **x**, you can often be familiar with concepts appearing in problems around **x + 200** once you've practiced enough at that level.

The goal isn't to collect ratings or titles. Ratings are a byproduct. Chase the moment when problems that once seemed daunting start feeling familiar.
